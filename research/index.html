Telling deliberate from spontaneous blinks on a webcam

Few-shot calibration for blink-based Morse text entry: a preregistered single-person case study, a check on public blink videos, a features-only dataset and an open-source browser system.

Research questions

RQ1 · Detection

On a consumer webcam (about 30 fps), how accurately can landmark-based detectors find deliberate blinks while ignoring spontaneous ones? How much does per-user calibration with k examples (k = 0, 1, 3, 5, 10) help?

RQ2 · Timing

What are the duration distributions of spontaneous blinks and deliberate short and long closures? What dot, dash and reject thresholds do they imply, and how much does the frame rate limit timing precision?

RQ3 · Text entry

What entry rate and error rate do novices reach, and how do they change over three short blocks of practice?

The hypotheses are written down in docs/preregistration.md before the first session; its commit date shows when. Any later change is listed there under “Deviations”, with its date.

Study design

Study A: blink detection benchmark

One participant, the author, in 6–8 sessions of about 12 minutes on at least four days, on a laptop webcam 50–70 cm away, in normal, bright and dim light. Lighting, glasses and the measured frame rate are recorded for each session. Results describe this one person, not people in general.

Study A protocol
Block Length What happens
Consent – Consent screen, with a separate opt-in for video
Calibration 1 min 15 s eyes open on a dot; 10 cued short and 10 cued long closures
Spontaneous 3 min Read a neutral text; natural blinks only
Deliberate 3 min 40 random cues (short, long, wink left, wink right), 2–4 s apart
Mixed 3 min Read, and respond to cues that appear in a corner

Ground truth. Every closure’s onset, offset and type is labelled by hand by the author, using cues only as hints. Two sessions are re-labelled a week later to report self-agreement (Cohen’s κ for type, mean onset difference in ms). Labelling time is reported.

Detectors compared

All five run offline on the same recorded features, with hysteresis and no smoothing buffer.

Detectors D1–D5
ID Signal Threshold
D1 Mean EAR of both eyes Fixed 0.21 (the original app)
D2 Mean EAR Per user, from k closures
D3 Blink blendshape, max of both eyes Fixed 0.5
D4 Blink blendshape Per user, from k closures
D5 Wink: one eye closed, the other open Per user (exploratory)

Calibrated threshold: open − 0.5 · (open − closed), from the median open level and the median of k closures’ deepest points.

Other people’s natural blinks

The fixed detectors (D1, D3) also run on Eyeblink8, a public set of 8 videos of 4 people with 408 hand-marked natural blinks, to see how often ordinary blinks would be typed as symbols. This check is not blind: it was partly looked at before the preregistration was written. A larger dataset (Researcher’s Night) has been requested for a blind test.

Metrics and statistics

  • Event-level precision, recall and F1 for deliberate closures (a match needs interval IoU ≥ 0.3).
  • False triggers per minute in the spontaneous (reading) block.
  • Duration error (detected minus labelled), compared with the frame interval.
  • Short/long classification accuracy and confusion matrix.
  • Per-session metrics, summarised as the mean with a 95% bootstrap interval over sessions (10,000 resamples). Paired detector comparisons use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. With 6–8 sessions, only large, consistent differences can be detected.
  • Few-shot calibration: for each session and each k, 20 random draws of k calibration closures; each fitted threshold is evaluated on that session’s other blocks.

Study B: text entry

The best detector from Study A, with thresholds fixed in advance from the Study A distributions. One session a day for five days: fifteen short phrases from the MacKenzie and Soukoreff (2003) set, in three blocks of five, with a Morse reference card on screen.

  • Entry rate in characters per minute (WPM = CPM / 5).
  • Character error rate from the minimum string distance, corrected and uncorrected.
  • Closures per character, including deletions.
  • Raw NASA-TLX workload after each block.

Limitations and threats to validity

The participant is one able-bodied adult, the author. The system has not been tested with people with motor impairments, and no clinical claim is made.

  • One participant. Intervals describe variation across one person’s sessions, not across people; tests have low power.
  • Participant, labeller and designer are the same person. He knows the hypotheses and labels his own data, which risks bias; self-agreement κ and the public data address this only in part.
  • Small public check. Eyeblink8 has only four people, and its analysis is not blind.
  • One device. One laptop camera.
  • Cued closures. Deliberate blinks are made on cue, not from a natural intent to write.
  • Lab task. The reading task is an in-the-lab stand-in for everyday use.
